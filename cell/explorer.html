Level 7 · m · flight reference

The hull

kg/m³ — the wall

air density at the 2,500 m working altitude

A vacuum shell heavier than this, per cubic metre it encloses, has no net lift at any size, ever. Lighter, and the hull can be grown until lift covers the whole ledger. Every level below this one exists to get under that number — the hull itself is just the array, continued until it is a vehicle. Machinery lives in ambient bays inside it, bolted to structure the cells pull up on; nothing operates in vacuum.

The ladder of structure-inside-structure is what closes the gap. Each level improves the strength-to-density exponent — (n+2)/(n+1), tending to linear (Lakes, Nature 361, 1993) — and it converges fast:

Level 2 is the design target. Beyond it the exponent gains shrink while every added level still costs joints, tolerance and inspection. The final ships are generatively designed to these specs — load-graded, built and maintained by machine — but the rule they follow is this table.

Level 6 · ~20 m · flight reference

Section & machinery bay

atm on the outer envelope

behind a graded band, instead of a full atmosphere

Sealed at every scale is the organising rule: sub-cells inside cells, cells inside sections, each level evacuated and sealed so no partition ever faces more than its own level's differential — and a breach is bounded by the smallest enclosure that contains it. A section is a cell of cells.

The pumped plenum is the same doctrine made active, and it is what this picture is really showing: a soft shell across the whole ship holding as much vacuum as its pumps can — lossy, never sealed — so that no interior cell operates against a full atmosphere. Statics still delivers one atmosphere to the array through the shell's mounts, so the plenum buys no lattice mass; what it buys is margin. A cell proven at a full atmosphere and operated at half of one runs at × crush margin, its permeation drive halves, a breach floods to half an atmosphere — and a pump failure is a slow drift back to the design case the cells were built for. Redundancy, not rescue.

The graded band is the boundary's version of the same idea. Stage the outermost % of volume across pressure steps and the envelope — the only surface that ever sees a full atmosphere — sees a tenth of it, and its film gets ten times lighter with it. That saving covers the band's gas: the whole arrangement nets % of net lift — roughly free in mass, paid for operationally by tying the band's trim to gas temperature. In bulk, the same idea fails: the gas costs lift everywhere while the deep lattice still carries its cumulative load — the vacuum core sees the full atmosphere no matter how many steps stand above it:

levelsstructuregas filmsnet lift

The ambient bay holds pumps, tanks and plant at one atmosphere inside the ship — ordinary machinery, ordinary maintenance, surrounded by the cells that carry it.

Open on this level: whether interior films are sized to contain a breach (0.347 kg/m³) or only to seal (nearly free, but a breach cascades) — the report calls that the sharpest unresolved trade in the design, and sealing at every scale is the lever that shrinks it.

Level 5 · 2 m cells · flight reference

The array

% of wall area carries nothing

interior walls have vacuum on both sides — Δp = 0

A monolithic vacuum balloon dies by shell buckling — a scale-free result no material escapes. Fill the volume with sealed cells instead and the atmosphere is held only at the boundary: the array is a cellular solid in hydrostatic compression, and its crushing strength is a well-posed engineering problem. Two honest caveats the analysis itself carries: it is the hollow-strut step that does most of the numerical work, and what cells specifically buy is the removal of the hull's shape penalty plus failure containment — which is the claim this level demonstrates.

Click a cell to flood it. A breach is a local load: the flooded cell's neighbours see one atmosphere across their shared walls, a factor-L concentration that costs L^⅔ in local density where it happens — not a redesign of the ship. At the level-2 flight target:

case×L^⅔kg/m³ local margin

Margins at the level-2 target ( kg/m³ with the envelope film). The demonstrable single-level design is under the wall before any breach — these margins are what the ladder buys.

The partition shape is the Kelvin cell — the truncated octahedron, BCC's Voronoi cell, the interlocking near-sphere. It carries % less film area than cubes at equal volume, and the analysis still charges the cube figure — conservative on purpose:

The skin wears its lattice — the brightened cell shows it. At half the cell pitch, the sub-lattice's nodes land exactly in the Kelvin surface: corner nodes in the hexagons, axis nodes in the squares (Kelvin is BCC's Voronoi cell, and the half-pitch octet grid contains the BCC points). The membrane is bonded at that regular grid of boundary nodes and its films bulge between them. A printed cube's corner nodes can never lie on a Kelvin surface — which is why the demonstrator's skin is a cube, and why the flight cell's lattice is generated to conform to its partition rather than tiled from the printed article.

Level 4 · mm · one sealed cell

The cell

litres of nothing

carbon cuts · printed joints · kg

A truncated octahedron: the shape that fills space with the least wall per unit volume. Eight hexagons, six squares, thirty-six edges — near-spherical, so the buckling radius is the cell's own, and space-filling, so cells seat face-to-face on flat mating lands and the array becomes the airframe rather than cargo inside one. Every face holds the sky out: tonnes-force on each of the eight hexagons and on each of the six squares — tonnes-force squeezing inward over the whole surface, forever, carried by tubes you can buy and joints you can print.

Two different jobs, and they nearly got confused. The interior octet is in pure compression — that is the easy part, and one 251 mm cut repeated 144 times does it. The boundary is not: the film does not push on the lattice, it pulls the edge frame sideways, and at a dihedral the two panels' tensions add instead of cancelling. Unbraced, that tore the rim off at atmospheres — less than half the load the cell exists to hold. The squares were already saved by their own in-plane ties; the hexagons had nothing, so each now carries radial spokes, six per face, at the same cut length as every primary.

Bracing pays twice, and the second time is bigger. Bending falls as the cube of the bracing pitch, so the spokes buy strength cheaply. They also buy back volume: every panel bulges inward and that dimple is open to the sky, so an unbraced cell quietly forfeits % of its own displacement — a fifth of the lift on anything meant to float. Spoked, %.

Every member is purchased carbon; every joint is printed and solid. kg of tube at × Euler margin even pinned, and kg of joints in Bambu PAHT-CF (the chopped-carbon nylon, kg/m³) — weighed from their own geometry, not budgeted. Each joint grips its tube three ways at once: a spigot inside the bore, a collar outside, both bottomed on a shoulder. Solid, because these parts live inside the vacuum and sparse infill is a virtual leak. Use the parts button to take the tubes away and look at the joinery alone.

Does this one float? No — and the reason is size, not material. In wound M60J-class this geometry is kg/m³ as a single article, buoyant at sea level; printed continuous fibre needs N=. The deeper rule is parity: at odd N a hexagon face contains no lattice site at all and must carry this whole rim-and-spoke apparatus, while at even N the face is full of them and the boundary structure simply disappears. The floater wants to be the N=2 article. This one is the bench article: it exists to be crushed, sealed, and pumped down.

The same geometry printed end to end in nylon — the variant that proves the printer chain rather than the physics — comes to kg against g of displaced air.

printed joints · families · one cell

The connectors

printed joints, families, and nothing else in the article is printed. Every one grows from the same signed distance field: spigots smooth-min-blended into a core, the bores and an annular seating slot cut afterwards, the flat mating lands truncated last so no blend can round one back off. Change a diameter and all regrow. Together they weigh g — integrated from each mesh that was written, not budgeted at a fraction of strut mass.

Capture is internal. The pipe slides over a hollow spigot and butts on a shoulder; the bore is the mandrel-formed surface on a roll-wrapped tube, which is why the spigot indexes there and the compliance lives outside it. mm of engagement gives mm² of glue line — MPa at the N a strut is asked for, × against a 10 MPa adhesive — and the spigot's own section carries MPa. E5 crushes one joint dry and one glued, because a uniformly evacuated shell is all compression and the dry result decides whether adhesive is needed at all.

The nozzle no longer sets the size of the article — it sets the size of these. When the members were printed too, a mm bead forced every dimension above it: wall, strut, cell. Buying the tube broke that chain and the article is now free to be whatever the structure wants; what the bead still governs is the joinery — minimum wall, the fit a printed spigot can hold, whether a feature needs support. perimeters of it make the mm wall these parts are built out of. They print solid, and not for strength: they live inside the vacuum, and sparse infill is a virtual leak that would bleed for weeks into the one measurement this article exists to take. Z is the governing direction — MPa and GPa between layers against MPa and GPa in plane — so every figure on this page uses the interlayer numbers, not the datasheet's lead line.

What separates the families is not the arm count, it is the angle. An annular slot is a tube of outer radius mm around each arm, and two slots at angle θ intersect until the slot starts beyond that radius over the sine of the half-angle. Every family but the centre has a ° pair somewhere, so their slots begin mm out; the cell centre's tightest pair opens to °, so its slots begin at mm. That is where a mm member stops being a member and starts being a cut. Nothing in a render shows it. It shows up on the bench as a pipe that fouls on the remains of its neighbour's slot and never reaches its seat.

Two more things these meshes are not yet honest about, stated rather than smoothed over. The nominal mm shoulder is not reachable: the printed collar ends mm from the node centre while the slot-clearance rule starts the seat at – mm, so the pipe lands on the blend and this page does not publish a bearing stress. And the crush ribs are subtracted by the slot over the whole engagement — at mm they are of the mm voxel these meshes were extracted at, and the fit clearance is of one. The shipped STLs are geometry of record for form, not for fit.

× the orthotropy toll

local buckling rides √(Eaxial·Ehoop), not the datasheet number

Roll-wrapped, not pultruded, and this is not a preference. A composite tube wall is not one material, it is fibres pointing somewhere. Local buckling of an orthotropic tube rides √(Ex·Eθ), so the governing modulus is Ex^¾·Eθ^¼ and the best [0/90] split — three quarters axial, one quarter hoop — delivers of the fibre's headline stiffness. That factor has been in this model since the day the orthotropic penalty was written. A pultrusion is all-axial fibre: buying one would contradict the physics the article is priced by, and it would split at the sockets, where a spigot presses outward on hoop fibre a pultrusion does not have. Specify a [0/±45/90] wall, roll-wrapped over a mandrel — the bore is then the mandrel-formed surface, which is why the spigot indexes on the bore and the compliance lives outside it. The model prices the tube as .

What the tube is asked for. N per strut, × on Euler even pinned, × socketed, × on stress. And the published Euler figure is the conservative one twice over: the model takes the whole centre-to-centre length as the free length, but the member is held at its shoulders, over the mm cut. On that length it is × — a factor of better, and not claimed anywhere the model is used.

The wall thickness is set by the co-critical rule: thin enough that local buckling and Euler arrive together, thick enough that somebody can wrap it. That proportion fixes the wall as a constant fraction of strut length — which is why the printer's thinnest reliable wall set the cell size before a single member was purchased, and the whole chain still runs backwards from the nozzle:

nozzlewallstrut cellencloses

co-critical here is R/t · the flight reference optimises to R/t · the tube we buy is R/t

At floating densities struts are so slender that Euler buckling governs, not strength — solid rods give φ^ scaling and nothing floats. Hollow tubes restore φ^, if they are proportioned so that the strut's Euler load and the wall's local buckling arrive together. A catalogue section is not that proportion, and this page will not pretend otherwise: at R/t the bought tube is far inside co-critical, so local buckling is nowhere near it and the wall is carrying mass it does not need. That is the right trade at demonstrator scale — a stock section beats an optimal one you cannot order — and it is a trade the flight article cannot make. The proportion, not the topology, is what the mass turns on. And this wall is where the next rung lives: make it a lattice — two thin skins on a micro-truss core — and the exponent moves from to , the difference between and kg/m³ (× over the wall).

Sourcing note, and it is prose: Rock West Composites sell roll-wrapped tube in these sections, including UHM grades. No catalogue number is committed here and no vendor figure enters the model — the sizes are the model's, and the only claim is that they are ordinary stock rather than a special order.

The skin

square metres of fabric, grams, and the last thing in this article that is not solved. hexagons at m² each — m², % of the surface — and squares at m², m². The material is Dyneema composite fabric: a UHMWPE fibre grid laminated between films, the same stock as ultralight tents, bought by the metre.

What it is sized for. g/m² on the hexagons, on the squares — about and microns of fibre and film. Neither is off a datasheet. The dihedral edge

This is the level's argument. Two panels meeting inside a flat face pull in opposite directions and their in-plane parts cancel; only twice the tension times sin α is left — N/m on a spoke. Two panels meeting across a dihedral do not cancel. The hexagon–hexagon dihedral is arccos(−⅓) = °, and the two tensions add along the bisector: N/m, a factor of . Missing that is what let the rim look survivable.

joints · flat lands

The mating land

Where the film is supposed to be bonded, and where cells seat face to face. of the printed joints carry one or more — on a square centre, on a hub, at a corner where one square and two hexagons meet — flat faces in all, truncated last in the SDF so no blend can round one back off.

A hub collects N of a hexagon's load and hands it to three props at N each. The printed pad the design once carried is gone — the manifest ships pad_r = — and the arithmetic is why: a disc only picks up what the film hands it around its own perimeter, so at mm across it could collect N, and it would have had to be mm across — wider than the hexagon — to take the hub's share. The spokes carry it. A pad was only ever local protection against a point support puncturing a film microns thick.

And nothing has been chosen to go on these lands. No adhesive, no bond-line width, no shear allowable, no peel case exists anywhere in this repository. The lands are designed; the bond is not. That is the honest and more interesting claim, and it is E6's job.

The bulge tax. Every panel dimples inward — mm on a spoked hexagon, on a square, on a bare hexagon — and that dimple is open to the sky, so the article displaces less air than its outline claims. Braced, the bulges swallow litres, % of the cell's litres. Unbraced, %. On the bench that is cosmetic. On a floater, where mass and displaced air are equal by construction, it is a fifth of the lift — a better argument for bracing than film mass ever was.

tonnes-force, standing on the fabric

N over m² of film and kg of tube

What is unresolved, and it is three things. E6 is the coupon that settles them, and none of it is softened here.

It is a membrane, not a barrier. The g/m² above is sized for strength. A real barrier laminate of the class this project has measured is several times heavier, and wrapped around a cell this size that is lift-negative on its own skin — deliberately, because the demonstrator is not meant to be flight representative. Only a deposited stack scales to every cell size, and nobody has a measured number for one on printed PAHT-CF. That is experiment E1, and it must be measured rather than argued. The arithmetic behind that comparison lives in research/notes/barrier-and-permeation.md, marked as arithmetic and under no checker, so this page does not put a figure on it.

The bulge is assumed, not achieved. Every number above takes the panel's dimple as of its half-span, and that needs a membrane strain several times what this fibre delivers at the working stress above. Either the film is pre-formed to its loaded dome before it is bonded, or the reachable bulge is far shallower and the film is several times heavier — which sinks it. The same strain would craze any inorganic barrier coating on first pump-down. That arithmetic is generated, in research/analysis/vacuum-cell.json under designPoint, but it is not exposed through the model this page reads, so the problem is stated and the figure is not.

The bond is undesigned, as the land card says. And Dyneema creeps under permanent load, so the spec is a heat-treated rigging grade or a Vectran laminate instead.

This is deferred on purpose. The structure is sized, gated and buildable; the skin is a fabric problem behind it, and pretending otherwise would be the one place this page lied.
