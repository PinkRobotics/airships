Pink Robotics — Airships · The build, staged

No cell floats.
The ship does.

For a season the question here was how to make one vacuum cell lighter than the air it displaces. The verified answer is that it never will be — and never needed to be. A cell is a wall unit. A ship is a machine built from them: a deep lattice shell carrying the sky's whole squeeze, a band of cells killing the pressure at the skin, and a core of engineered nothing doing the lifting. Seven levels, from sintered titanium and rolled carbon to a working ship on a fire line — this page is the blueprint the explorer follows.

Level 1 · The catalog

Everything begins as powder, fibre, or film: titanium sintered into split clamshells that clamp around their members, carbon rolled onto mandrels, membrane pressed into its dome before it ever meets a frame. These are the parts — each one browsable below, each carrying its own honesty chip. Tabs switch the family; the arrows — or a click on any bore in the strip — walk the catalog.

Level 2 · The cell

One Kelvin cell: the first buildable block, deliberately not lighter than air. It assembles from the catalog's parts — tubes cut to a measured saw table, titanium clamps closed around every meeting of members, the formed film drawn over the frame — then takes its vacuum and proves every joint and film load the ship will reuse a hundred thousand times.

Level 3 · The band

Cells joined shoulder to shoulder, one cell deep — and one layer is the design, not a thrift: the pressure a band can kill grows with the cube of its cell span, so a single span already absorbs the entire atmosphere, and stacked bands were measured to buy no mass at all. Depth for stiffness is the grid's job; the band spends its cells where they work.

Redundancy is not more layers — it is that every cell is its own sealed vessel. Between healthy neighbours an interior film has vacuum on both sides and carries nothing; the moment one cell is holed, the films around it pick up the differential and the loss stays one cell big — a valve closes, and the ship flies on having lost the lift of one cell's own small volume. The swap itself is maintenance-window work: landed with the interior re-pressurised (pumping back down costs the generators a few hours), or aloft behind a clamped cofferdam that leans on the neighbours exactly the way a cell does — never through an open hole. What one wall of cells cannot survive alone is a spreading cascade, and that policy — including a sacrificial outer-gap variant — is an open study, flagged as such.

Level 4 · The grid & skins

The big struts arrive. The band can kill the atmosphere locally but cannot carry the ship's global squeeze — that belongs to a deep sandwich shell: two faces of fat carbon chord pipe held metres apart by web diagonals, joined by the same clamped titanium joints as the cell, only larger. The hoop chords are the ring frames. The sealed band wraps just outside the outer face, pressed onto it by the sky; the weather jacket closes over it, and a whisper of a void skin bounds the core within.

The interplay is a clean division of labour. Follow the load: the film drums the sky into each cell's rims; one cell's frame gathers about tonnes of inward push; its face presses that push straight into the outer wall and the webs walk it down; and the rings turn twenty thousand small pushes into one great hoop of compression around the barrel. In return the grid gives every cell a stiff foundation — inboard of the band the air is a hundredth of an atmosphere, so no cell is ever asked to carry the ship — only its own patch of sky — and no chord ever meets the raw atmosphere. The cells stay one layer deep; the sandwich's metres of depth are empty truss, not stacked cells — that space is where the shell gets its bending stiffness, the way an I-beam gets its from the gap between flanges.

And what holds a cell up? On the bench, nothing has to: the sky pushes on every face and every push cancels its opposite through the frame. A band cell has lost half its sky — the outer face keeps its push, the balancing push from behind is gone — so the grid stands in for it: each cell simply sits face-down on the outer wall just inside it — the seat embedded in the face, compression over millimetres, which cannot buckle — and that bearing supplies exactly the reaction the back-side atmosphere used to. Sky on one face, the wall on the other — the through-loaded state the band's crush law is written for. Nothing ever pushes back from inside the void: the near side of the hull is balanced by the far side, the load carried around the emptiness by the rings as hoop compression — a keystone arch, closed on itself.

Seen in section, the ship is skin and bones. The bones are a single endoskeleton — a two-walled truss, heavy chords in both walls laced by the webs, because a single-walled ring would have no depth to resist going out-of-round. The skin is the sealed wall of evacuated cells wrapped around it, pressed onto the bones by the sky itself. Small, delicate, light on the outside; big, strong, heavy inside; nothing in the middle. And the boundary sits at the largest radius the ship owns, so everything inboard — webs, both walls, the whole depth of the machine — lives in vacuum and counts as lift.

The junction that forced this arrangement is worth recording. Webs must lace the two truss walls, and a sealed wall dislikes being crossed — so the ruling puts the wall outside everything it would otherwise have to dodge. Nothing penetrates it; every strut lives behind it, in the lift; and the loaded membrane faces nothing but sky — every attachment lands on the unloaded inner side, at film corners that already terminate sealed on their joints.

Level 5 · Closure provisional name

The hull closes, the pumps run down, and the machine becomes the thing the whole site is about: a vessel whose cargo is nothing, pressed on every side by the entire sky, weighing less than the air it pushed out of the way. Lift is fixed by geometry; everything else is a fight to keep structure under it.

Level 6 · Equipment & paint

Rotors, tanks, avionics, the winch, the livery — everything the residual lift exists to buy. On the ledger these are named lines held open on purpose: the payload axis. The design rule stays what it has always been here — every line named, none silently zero — and the paint goes on last, on the one skin that never feels the atmosphere.
