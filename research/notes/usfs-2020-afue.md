# USDA Forest Service (2020) — Aerial Firefighting Use and Effectiveness (AFUE) Final Report

US Government work. Read in full.

## What it establishes

AFUE is the only large-scale field observation of what aerial drops actually do. Dedicated
firefighting crews rode 272 incidents across 18 states from 2015 to 2018 and recorded, drop by
drop, what the drop was *for* and what happened: 18,929 helicopter drops, 3,303 scooper drops and
5,379 airtanker retardant drops.

Two metrics come out of it. Interaction Percentage is the share of drops that touched fire at all;
Probability of Success is effective drops divided by drops with known, interacting outcomes
(p. 15); it is not the share of all released loads that succeeded. Across everything,
IP was 89% and POS 0.82. The pattern by platform is the interesting part: helicopters and scoopers
were mostly used to reduce fire intensity and did it well (POS 0.81–0.96), while SEATs, LATs and
VLATs were used to halt fire spread and build aerial line, where POS falls to **0.55–0.67**. The
largest aircraft also had the highest rate of unknown outcomes, because nobody could see whether
the drop worked.

For the sampled airtanker retardant drops, estimated POS was **0.72 with ground engagement
and 0.56 without** (p. 32, Fig. 20), using the known, interacting-outcome denominator.
The study used longitudinal, non-random sampling (p. 9). Ground engagement concerns crews
on the ground, not whether the aircraft has a pilot. Among recorded outcomes without ground
engagement, *not effective* was the most common individual category, at 26%; that outcome
distribution has a different denominator from POS.

## Why it cuts against us

This project's water-rate arithmetic describes logistics. AFUE's observational contrast
motivates coordinating with ground crews; it does not isolate a causal effect of crews or
assign a success probability to this unbuilt fleet. Aircraft, liquid, task and encounter
conditions would need matched evidence before any such transfer. The fleet should work for
ground crews, and useful treatment must be measured separately from tank discharge.

It also quietly refuses our metric. AFUE never measures tonnes and never measures fire
extinguished; "extinguish fire/spot fire" appears in its own objective list as "generally a rare
occasion, usually a small area, and likely a fine/flashy fuel". The framework the Forest Service
built after being told by the GAO that it had no effectiveness data is objectives-against-outcomes,
per drop. Volume delivered is not in it. If the best available evidence declines to treat tonnage
as the measure of aerial suppression, our `tph` figure is not a performance claim — it is a
logistics claim wearing one.

## Where it does not support what we would like

It cannot settle the energy comparison the README asks for. AFUE reports no cost, no fuel burn and
no energy per tonne, so "is 4–13 kWh per delivered tonne better than conventional suppression?"
has no answer in this document, and we should stop implying one exists.

Nor is POS an outcome measure for the fire. It is a judgement, made in the field from observation
and radio traffic, about whether one drop did what that drop was asked to do — the report is
explicit that crews translated phrases such as "it hung it up for long enough" into a domain list.
Most drops in the study happened on large fires already beyond initial attack, so the sample is
weighted towards the hardest conditions. And nothing in it describes an aircraft remotely like
ours: the largest platform observed carries a load measured in tens of tonnes, not thousands.
