Pink Robotics · Autonomous fleet monitor · how it works

Live fires. Imagined machines. Testable arithmetic.

The fire locations, sizes and perimeters below are current British Columbia public data. The fleet is simulated: sixteen imagined hulls — ten P-100s, five P-1000s, one P-10000 — shared across the worst out-of-control fires, cycling between mapped water and the fire line while the uncovered fires wait; fires that crews already hold are monitored, not swarmed. The monitor does not predict that any fire would be extinguished. It shows what the proposed machines would have to do, how often they could do it, and what each cycle would cost in mass, force, time and energy.

No flightworthy vacuum airship exists — not ours, not anyone's. The physics case and the first falsifiable milestone are on the main page; this page is the idea run forward, in public, against today's fires.

One cycle, end to end

Concept

The vehicle is best described as a mass-adaptive electric aircraft built around permanent vacuum lift. The vacuum cells stay evacuated; they are structure, not machinery. Everything the ship does, it does by changing the rest of its mass and force budget: water in, water out, nitrogen liquefied from the air as ballast, rotors, aerodynamics, and altitude. The characteristic wildfire mission is one loop of that budget:

Drop low. Escape upward. Return through the best wind layer. Force the vehicle into hose range. Let the next load of water pull it down.

Figure 1 The mission cycle: six phases — the hose rides the approaches, and the site is pure pumping.
At the water: the ship never touches the lake — an expendable pump pod does.

A vehicle this large and this structurally light should be assumed intolerant of contact — with water, waves, trees, docks or debris. So it does not land and it does not skim. It holds position at altitude and lowers a long hose with a submerged intake pod: redundant electric pumps at the wet end (a pump on board could not suction-lift a water column hundreds of metres), screens, cameras, depth sensing, and an emergency release. The aircraft never needs to touch the lake. The expendable, replaceable pump pod reaches the water; the fragile lifting structure remains safely above it.

As water is pushed up the hose, the ship gets heavier, its net positive buoyancy falls, and the downforce it was spending to hold itself low is progressively handed over to the payload itself. It only ever needs to force itself into hose range — incoming water does the rest of the pulling down.

At the fire: the release is also the escape manoeuvre.

The approach is made outside the worst convection where possible, toward a delivery point on the fire perimeter facing the water source — not the centre of the burning area. Water leaves from many distributed tanks, symmetrically, around the centre of buoyancy. Dropping a full payload instantly makes the ship payload-weight buoyant, and the design treats that as a feature: the surplus lift becomes a deliberate, pre-planned climb away from the dangerous low-altitude environment, rather than a force to be fought.

The Mind chooses the post-drop buoyancy in advance — through retained water, nitrogen ballast already aboard, thrust and release timing — so the climb is controlled, not ballistic. A full emergency dump produces a much larger climb than a normal delivery, and that too is a planned case.

Getting back down: nitrogen, rotors, and the next load of water.

After the drop the ship is light by design, and something has to bring it back to hose range. Three levers, used together: the cryogenic plant liquefies nitrogen from the surrounding air during the return leg, adding dense ballast that costs energy but no volume; the large vectorable rotors can produce temporary downforce; and once the hose is in the water, every tonne pumped aboard replaces a tonne of forced descent. Liquid nitrogen is not only ballast. It is a controllable mass state, a cold reservoir, and a partially recoverable battery — warmed back through heat exchangers and expanded through a turbine, it returns a fraction of the electricity it cost, and that fraction is a number this page shows rather than hides.

Why nitrogen — and not compressed air, or the liquid air that would be cheaper to make.

The ballast system has one job: add tonnes the Mind can dial, aboard a vehicle that is mostly made of nothing. That sets the requirements. The store has to be dense at atmospheric pressure, because every cubic metre ballast occupies is a cubic metre of lift spent housing it. It has to be harvestable from the air mid-flight, because the ship cannot land to take ballast on. And it has to be safe inside a printed polymer and carbon-fibre airframe whose destination is a fire.

Compressed air fails on mass. A tank strong enough to hold a tonne of air at pressure is itself on the order of a tonne of structure — pressure vessels pay for their contents in wall. On a vehicle whose entire structural allowance is about 6.5 kg/m², that is buying mass with the one budget the project cannot spare. A cryogenic liquid sits at one atmosphere in a thin insulated tank: 808 kg of nitrogen per cubic metre, and the tank is a rounding error.

Liquid air is the tempting one. Skip the separation step and liquefaction gets meaningfully cheaper per kilogram — that is exactly the economy grid-scale liquid-air energy storage runs on. But liquid air is about 21% oxygen, and it does not stay 21%. Nitrogen boils at 77 K, oxygen at 90 K, so a standing tank boils its nitrogen off first and the liquid that remains enriches toward liquid oxygen — which is why cryogenic practice treats aged liquid air as LOX. Aboard this vehicle that property is disqualifying three times over:

  • Oxygen-enriched cryogen soaked into organic material — insulation, resin, carbon fibre — forms impact-sensitive, explosive combinations. This airframe is largely printed polymer and carbon fibre.
  • A leak wets the structure with oxidizer. The structure is the fuel.
  • The operating environment is a wildfire. Venting an oxygen-enriching liquid over the one thing the mission exists to smother is not a failure mode anyone should have to analyse.

Nitrogen is the boring four-fifths of every breath the intake takes. It is inert: a leak is nothing, a vent near the fire is nothing, boil-off stays pure instead of concentrating a hazard, and ground handling is routine industrial practice rather than LOX discipline. The separation column hands the oxygen straight back to the sky rather than storing it, and its energy cost is already priced into the 0.45 kWh/kg dial in section 04. The modest discount liquid air offers is the price of not flying a slowly concentrating oxidizer through a fire in a flammable airframe — a bad trade at any efficiency. (Argon would be denser ballast still, and just as inert, but air offers only one percent of it; nitrogen is what the sky actually stocks.)

Three operating doctrines — the cycle has no single right answer.

Rapid response spends energy to buy time: faster transits, aggressive rotor downforce instead of waiting for nitrogen, batteries and generators run hard, more tonnes per hour at more kilowatt-hours per tonne. Endurance spends time to buy energy: favourable wind layers, a longer natural climb, nitrogen produced patiently in transit, solar and steady generation doing more of the work. Balanced sits between them and is the monitor's default. Switch modes in the arithmetic section and every mission on the map recomputes.

The routing question underneath all three is the project's central one: which three-dimensional atmospheric trajectory costs the least energy while keeping this aircraft within its structural envelope? The monitor currently draws nominal routes — live vertical-wind optimization is not active, and the page says so wherever it matters.

The machines

Conceptual — none exist

Three reference scales, named for their payload in tonnes of water. The P-100 is the vehicle whose mass ledger is worked through on the main page; the larger two are the same proportions scaled up, because the physics rewards size: lift grows with volume while structure grows closer to surface. Every number on these cards is a demonstration assumption — illustrative same-proportion scaling, not a finalized vehicle geometry or structural result.

Figure 2 All three, drawn against the Hindenburg, a 747, and the Lions Gate Bridge.
Figure 3 Inside the hull: a sponge that flies, not a balloon that strains.
Power: a redundant high-voltage electric ship, fuel-agnostic at the generator boundary.

Solar across the upper surface for persistent background power; batteries for gust response, emergency manoeuvring and load buffering; dense liquid fuel through generator sets for the heavy work; nitrogen expansion giving back part of what the cryogenic plant spent; and shore power where an equipped water source offers it. Early large vehicles might burn conventional fuel because dense liquid energy is operationally straightforward; a mature fleet could use sustainable aviation fuel or synthetic hydrocarbons without changing the architecture — the vehicle is electric, and fuel-agnostic at the generator boundary.

The fleet is an ecology, not one universal machine: smaller autonomous logistics aircraft rendezvous with the large ships to transfer fuel, pump pods, spare modules and repair robots, which is how a vehicle that cannot land stays in service.

A fail-safe worth naming: abandon the mission, stop fighting the wind, shed discretionary mass, drift toward calmer and sunnier air, orient for solar, shut down everything nonessential, and recover. Not a claim that the ship can always climb over weather — severe convection goes higher than any of these vehicles — but a default that fails toward daylight rather than toward the ground.

The arithmetic, with its dials showing

Demonstration model

Every number the monitor displays comes from the model on this page — first-order physics, no CFD, no flight data, because there is no flight. The assumptions are dials, not secrets: move them and every mission on the map recomputes. Reset returns to the balanced defaults. Water delivered is not fire extinguished — nothing here converts tonnes per hour into hectares saved, because no such conversion honestly exists at this stage.

The throughput identity — the only equation most readers need.
tonnes / hour = payload × 60 / cycle-minutes

A 100-tonne aircraft on a 10-minute cycle is 6 drops an hour: 600 tonnes, 600,000 litres. Everything else on this page exists to justify the cycle-minutes honestly — and the cycle, not the payload, is where the argument lives. One refinement the monitor applies: when tanks and rotors together cannot cover a ship's post-drop buoyancy, the Mind retains part of the payload as descent ballast and only the rest counts as delivered — on the largest class that retention is substantial, and cryogenic capacity becomes the visible constraint.

Pumping: why filling takes minutes, not seconds.
P_pump = ρ · g · Q · h / η

Lifting water hundreds of metres up a hose is honest work: at the P-100's 0.5 m³/s through a 250 m head at 75% efficiency, about 1.6 MW while filling. The pumps live at the bottom of the hose because no pump at the top could suction-lift that column.

Transit: drag power at airship speeds.
P_drag = ½ · ρ · C_d · A · v³ / η_prop

Cube law: the reason these are 90–130 km/h vehicles and not 300 km/h vehicles. C_d and frontal area are configured assumptions shown in the panel above; the model is an idealized first-order estimate, not aerodynamics.

Descent authority: the actuator-disk estimate.
P_ideal = T^1.5 / √(2 · ρ · A_disk)

How much power it costs rotors of a given total disk area to push down with force T — divided by an overall efficiency and used whenever the ship must descend while still buoyant. This is the ideal momentum-theory floor; real installed performance would be worse.

The nitrogen battery: charge, hold, partially recover.
E_charge = m_LN₂ × e_liq   ·   E_back = E_charge × η_rt

Liquefying nitrogen from the air costs a configured 0.45 kWh/kg (adjustable 0.30–0.80); expansion recovers a configured 50% round trip (adjustable 35–60%). Both are demonstration assumptions, not specifications for an airborne plant, and both are dials above precisely because they materially move the answer. In endurance mode the cryogenic plant is usually the cycle's bottleneck — the monitor flags it when it is. Why the working fluid is nitrogen rather than cheaper-to-liquefy air is a safety argument, made in section 02.

The Minds, and what they are not allowed to do

Architecture

The Mind proposes and plans; deterministic systems decide what physical commands are permitted.

This is the same split Pink Robotics commits to everywhere a learned system touches the physical world, and it is already how the software layer that exists is governed. Applied to the fleet, it becomes a hierarchy: humans hold objectives, boundaries and stop authority; a strategic Mind allocates the fleet and escalates uncertainty; regional Minds deconflict airspace and shared water; each Vehicle Mind routes, budgets mass and energy, and explains itself — and none of them, at any layer, addresses an actuator directly. A verified deterministic kernel enforces the flight and structural envelope, terrain and water clearance, convection exclusion, communications-loss behaviour, emergency release, and a human stop that cannot be silently overridden.

Before the fire

The larger claim

Direct attack on a burning fire is the demonstration, not necessarily the mission. The strongest intervention may happen before ignition: moving water during wet seasons, sustaining drought-stressed regions, wetting critical corridors ahead of forecast fire weather, and delivering winter moisture to accumulate as snowpack. A fleet that can move a river is a climate-adaptation tool that happens to also be a firefighting tool.

What that looks like operationally.
  • Pre-wetting community protection corridors and strategic fuel breaks, repeatedly, on forecast.
  • Raising soil and fuel moisture around towns before the season peaks; protecting evacuation routes and critical infrastructure during it.
  • Supplying remote reservoirs, emergency livestock and community water, and acute drought relief for crops.
  • Moving water away from flood-threatened basins — the same vehicle, run in reverse of the drought case.
  • Winter high-altitude release intended to fall and accumulate as snow; long-term watershed recharge.
  • Delivering pumps, hoses, ground robots and communications gear — a hundred-tonne crane that never needs a road.
The atmospheric question, stated carefully.

Delivering water is making rain, to a degree. Water placement alters local humidity, evaporation, latent heat and surface moisture, and under favourable conditions might influence precipitation. The ability to create, steer or control weather systems is not established, and this project does not claim it. The commitment is the same one the main page makes: treat weather influence as a research obligation — model it, measure it, govern it, and stop when effects are uncertain. And plainly: this is climate adaptation and disaster infrastructure. It removes no carbon and licenses no emissions.

What this page is not claiming

  • No flightworthy vacuum airship exists — not ours, not anyone's. Pink Robotics has not demonstrated the first milestone, a complete positively buoyant evacuated cell.
  • The P-100, P-1000 and P-10000 are conceptual. Their dimensions are geometric scaling; their pump, cryogenic, propulsion and generator systems have not been designed as integrated aircraft.
  • Water delivered is not fire suppressed. This page models no fire behaviour and predicts no outcomes; simulated deliveries continue whether or not a real fire grows or is contained.
  • Mapped water surface area is not usable volume, legal access, ecological safety or permission to withdraw. Source selection here is a geometry exercise.
  • Transit times use one live 850 hPa wind sample per route when available; the routes and altitude profiles are nominal, not weather-optimized, and the force and power figures are first-order estimates, not engineering analysis.
  • The assumptions and the code that computes from them may contain mistakes or plain bugs. We publish both so they can be checked, and we correct what is found — but the whole simulation is a demonstration of the idea, not a certified model.
  • Nothing here is emergency information. Regulations, water rights, Indigenous rights and title, airspace coordination, incident command and ecological effects are all unresolved, and no aviation, environmental or emergency authority has reviewed this concept.
  • Weather influence is a research question, not a capability. And this is adaptation infrastructure — it removes no carbon.
  • Pink Robotics is one person. The way to make any of this real starts small: one sealed cell that floats. That milestone, and the gates after it, are on the main page.
